# Visual benchmark

The permanent quality bar for every Saanjh project. It was distilled from the
five reference images in this folder. Use it to judge direction and finished
work; **never** use it as a template. The references show a level of
quality, not a look to reproduce.

> **Benchmark:** cinematic + luxurious + immersive + sophisticated +
> interactive + refined + human-designed.

---

## 0. Scope and honesty about the source material

- There are **five** static images, 617–736 px wide, mostly
  Dribbble/Pinterest-style hero shots. Only R1 appears to be a screenshot of
  a live site (it shows the cursor, a video play control and a real footer).
- Every image is a **hero section only**. None of them show inner pages, the
  mobile layout, content density, forms, or how a narrative develops below
  the fold.
- **Motion, scrolling, transitions and video cannot be observed** in stills.
  Where this document discusses them, it is **inferring** what each
  composition implies or demands. Those inferences are marked _(inferred)_.
- The references are not flawless. Where one shows a weakness (clutter,
  cliché, lost legibility), this document says so, and the lesson is to
  take the strength and leave the weakness.
- The rules about **avoiding generic, template and AI-generated patterns**
  (§4) are general principles. They apply to every project whether or not a
  given pattern appears in these five images.

## 2. Analysis by dimension

### 2.1 Overall visual atmosphere

- Each reference commits to **one atmosphere** and holds it everywhere:
  cosmic void (R1), brooding weather (R2), calm tonal sage (R3), clinical
  studio light (R4), twilight dream (R5).
- The atmosphere is carried by **light**, not decoration: storm light in R2,
  amber window glow in R3, the orange visor glow in R4, a horizon glow in R5.
  Light implies a time of day, a place and a feeling.
- The best ones feel **quiet**. Luxury lowers its voice; nothing shouts
  except the one thing meant to.

### 2.2 Luxury level

- R1 and R2 feel the most expensive **because they are the most decisive**:
  few elements, each one placed with intent.
- R4 has production gloss but loses value through clutter; R3 loses some in
  its template-like lower band.
- In general, sites that look polished at first glance but are built from
  interchangeable parts **cheapen on inspection**.
- **Lesson:** perceived value comes from _specificity and restraint_, not
  from production gloss. Gloss is now cheap; judgement is not.

### 2.3 Typography

- **Scale contrast is the signature move.** R2's "TERRA" and R4's wordmark
  are architectural, bigger than the objects around them, while body copy
  stays small (roughly 11–13 px equivalent) and quiet. There is almost
  nothing at medium size.
- **Voice mixing within a headline:** a roman/italic switch (R3 "_Your
  Home_"), a sans + script pairing (R2 "TERRA _cotta_"), or a colour shift on
  one word (R5 "reality."). One switch per headline, never more.
- **Weight as tone:** thin/light display type reads refined (R3, R5). Heavy,
  tightly tracked sans reads confident (R4). Monospace reads technical and
  cultish (R1). The choice of typeface _is_ the brand statement.
- **Small caps and tracking** for labels and navigation (R5: "WORKS ·
  SERVICES…", "SCROLL") create hierarchy without adding size.
- **Type as image:** in R2 and R4 the headline is a compositional object
  that is layered, cropped and overlapped, not a text block placed on a layout.
- **Warnings:** R4's headline is truncated by the figure ("mat…"), which
  damages legibility and meaning. Overlap must never destroy the message.
  In general, a heavy geometric sans with a gradient-filled accent word is a
  stock "premium template" move.

### 2.4 Layout and composition

- **Two dominant patterns:**
  1. **Split hero** (R3, R5): text left on roughly 40% of the width, hero
     object right. It works, but it is the most common layout on the web.
     It needs a strong object and atmosphere to stay premium.
  2. **Centre-stage monument** (R1, R2, R4): one object or phrase dominates
     the centre. This is more cinematic and more distinctive.
- **The strongest compositions have one focal point and a clear reading
  path**: R1 (fingertip → fingertip), R2 (TERRA → house → cotta). R4 has at
  least eight competing focal zones.
- **Cropping creates scale:** R1's hands enter from the frame edges; R2's
  letters are cut by the house. What is implied beyond the frame makes the
  world feel bigger than the viewport.
- **Diagonals and gestures:** R1's reaching diagonal and R5's receding
  water path create tension and direction that a static grid cannot.
- **Edge furniture** (socials, scroll cues, page numbers, metadata) sits in
  the margins at small size, framing the stage like a film's title card.

### 2.5 Spacing

- **Negative space is the main material in R1, R3 and R5**: large calm
  fields with small, precise clusters of content.
- **Tight internal spacing, loose external spacing:** content clusters
  (R2's data card, R5's copy block) are compact, while the gaps between
  clusters are large. This contrast reads as intentional.
- **Crowding is the most common failure:** R4 and R3's lower band feel
  busier and less expensive for exactly this reason.
- Margins are generous and consistent; the navigation sits well inside the
  frame, never jammed against the edge.

### 2.6 Colour

Measured, not guessed: in every reference **most of the image (roughly
75–95% of pixels) is near-neutral** (charcoal, grey, sage-grey, slate).

| Ref | Base                   | Accent (small area)            |
| --- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| R1  | Near-black (~76%)      | Coral/terracotta wireframe     |
| R2  | Storm greys, charcoal  | Rust grass, warm cream type    |
| R3  | Sage-grey monochrome   | Amber window glow              |
| R4  | Pale cool grey + black | One orange visor               |
| R5  | Slate blue-grey        | Warm gold (one word + horizon) |

- **Rule: a restrained, low-saturation field with one warm accent** used on
  very few things, which are exactly the things that matter.
- Every accent is **warm** (coral, rust, amber, orange, gold). Warmth
  against a cool or neutral field creates emotional focus.
- The **accent often appears in the imagery and the type together** (R5's
  gold horizon and gold "reality."; R3's amber windows), tying art direction
  and UI together.
- Tonal or monochrome worlds (R3) read as highly designed because they are
  hard to hold.

### 2.7 Imagery

- **The hero image is the concept, not decoration.** R1's hands _are_ the
  product idea (human ↔ machine). R2's house _is_ the portfolio. R3's
  glowing house _is_ the smart home.
- **Clever, concept-driven images** are the highest-value moves: the
  art-historical quote (R1), letters parted by the building (R2).
- **Mixing real and rendered** creates intrigue: a photographed hand
  against a wireframe hand (R1); a real building photo interleaved with type (R2).
- **Grading unifies:** every image is colour-graded into the site's palette.
  Nothing looks like raw stock.
- **Warnings (general):** floating spheres, decorative potted plants and
  plinths are 3D-render filler, now strongly associated with templates and
  AI. Never use a third-party brand's trademark or product as the hero; it
  is legally risky and borrows another brand's value instead of building
  the client's own.

### 2.8 Video _(inferred)_

- R1 is the only reference with explicit video (a play control over the
  scene). It gates the product story behind a single, confident action.
- The compositions of R1, R2 and R5 are **cinematic stills**. They read as
  the first frame of a film and invite ambient motion: drifting stars,
  moving storm clouds, rippling water.
- Implied rule: video should be **ambient, graded and loop-seamless** (a
  living photograph), or a **deliberate, user-initiated** story. It should
  never be both at once, and never auto-play with sound.

### 2.9 3D

- **Three distinct uses of 3D appear:**
  1. **Stylised wireframe** (R1): signals technology and "the digital" and
     is cheap to render. It is a concept, not a showpiece.
  2. **Product/architectural hero render** (R3): a soft-lit, tactile object
     that could become an interactive or rotatable scene.
  3. **Surreal environment** (R5): world-building. It is atmospheric but
     the most prone to cliché.
- The best 3D is **integrated into a concept** (R1), not added as a
  showcase. R3's house works because it literally _is_ the product (a
  controllable home).
- 3D objects are **lit to match the page's atmosphere** (R3's amber interior
  light against a sage world). Mismatched lighting is what makes 3D look
  pasted on.

### 2.10 Depth and layering

- **Depth is the most consistent premium technique across the set.**
- **Type sandwiched between image layers** (R2: background sky → TERRA →
  house → "cotta"; R4: wordmark → figure → UI). This is the clearest
  "designed, not assembled" signal in the references.
- **Atmospheric perspective** (R5's hazy far clouds; R2's misty hills)
  creates distance.
- **Soft contact shadows and floor reflections** (R3's shadow; R5's water)
  ground objects in a real space.
- **Glass surfaces:** R2 uses one small, purposeful frosted data card as a
  mid-layer. In general, glass used as the entire aesthetic (glass on glass,
  frosted panels everywhere) tips a design into template territory.

### 2.11 Animation _(inferred)_

The stills imply what their motion would have to be:

- **R2:** letters rising from behind the house, the house settling
  forward, the script "cotta" drawn in with a pen stroke, hotspots pulsing
  once. This is choreographed layer by layer to reveal the depth.
- **R1:** the hands slowly closing the gap, driven by scroll or cursor,
  with the wireframe resolving. The motion carries the narrative.
- **R4/R5:** slow, heavy parallax between the figure or monolith and the
  typographic plane.
- Implied rule: **motion reveals the composition's layers and concept**.
  It is slow, weighted and eased (cinematic), never bouncy or uniform.
  Everything that moves has a reason to.

### 2.12 Scrolling _(inferred)_

- R5 and R4 carry explicit **scroll cues** (a "SCROLL" label with a fine
  line; "Scroll down"). They promise that the page continues as a
  narrative.
- Implied: **scroll as a timeline** that scrubs the hero scene (the gap
  closing between the hands, the camera moving through the arch in R5),
  then hands off to content chapters.
- The heroes are full-bleed single viewports, which implies **sectioned,
  paced storytelling** rather than an endless feed of cards.

### 2.13 Transitions _(inferred)_

- R2's project-data card and hotspots imply a **portfolio with case
  studies**: transitions between projects should carry continuity (the
  image expanding into the next page; shared elements via Flip).
- R4's carousel card ("Design to explore", with arrows) implies in-hero
  content transitions.
- Implied rule: **transitions preserve spatial continuity**. Things should
  move to where they are going, not cut or fade generically.

### 2.14 Interaction

- **Minimal visible UI; high-quality touchpoints:** R1 has only a play
  button and one outlined "join waitlist" control. That confidence is itself
  a luxury signal.
- **Hotspots** (R2) invite exploration of the image itself.
- **Custom, considered controls:** pill navigation (R2), a circular arrow
  button (R5), an icon + label CTA (R3), a split icon/text button (R4).
  Every control is designed; none look like browser or framework defaults.
- **Cursor as a participant** _(inferred)_: R1's composition begs for the
  cursor to _be_ the reaching finger, with the page responding to presence.
- **Warning:** R4's live clock, ratings, partner strip and carousel are
  interaction clutter. They are evidence of "more", not of care.

### 2.15 Visual storytelling

- **Every strong reference is a one-line story in a single frame:**
  - R1: the spark of creation, from human to digital.
  - R2: shelter against a wild landscape.
  - R3: a home glowing with life, controlled with calm.
  - R5: stepping through a threshold beyond reality.
- **Headline and image make the same argument** (R5's "beyond reality" and
  the impossible arch; R3's "control your home" and the glowing house).
  When they diverge, the page reads as assembled.
- **Cultural and art-historical references** (R1's Michelangelo) give depth
  and let the audience feel clever for recognising them.

## 3. What makes them feel expensive

1. **One idea, fully committed**, with no hedging or second concept
   competing (R1, R2).
2. **Extreme scale contrast** between monumental display type or objects
   and tiny, precise supporting text.
3. **A disciplined palette**: a neutral field with one warm accent, used
   sparingly and echoed in both the imagery and the type.
4. **Light as the art direction**: a specific time of day, weather and
   source, graded consistently.
5. **Real depth**: layers of image, type and UI interleaved, with type
   _behind_ subjects, atmospheric haze and grounded shadows.
6. **Negative space** used as a material, not as leftovers.
7. **Custom details** in every control, including buttons, nav, cursor,
   scroll cues and metadata.
8. **Specificity**: content, imagery and data only this client could have
   (R2's real architect names, area, year and location).
9. **Restraint**: fewer elements, each one intentional.

## 4. What makes them feel human-designed, and what to avoid

**Human signals seen in R1 and R2:**

- A **concept with a point of view** (the Creation-of-Adam quote, the
  "shelter in the landscape" metaphor): an idea, not a style.
- **Deliberate rule-breaking with control**: cropped letters, type behind
  image, a script against a grotesk. The risks are taken on purpose, in one
  place.
- **Real content**: real names, places, metrics, a real email. Specific
  and imperfect.
- **Asymmetry and tension**: a diagonal reach, an off-centre house.
- **Editorial judgement**: what was _left out_ is visible.

**Generic, template and AI-generated tells to avoid on every project.**
These are general principles. Some appear in the references (noted); the
rest are common patterns that the benchmark rejects outright.

- Floating spheres, orbs and pebbles with no meaning.
- A decorative potted plant or plinth in the corner of a 3D scene.
- Frosted glass as the whole aesthetic rather than one purposeful layer.
- A gradient-filled accent word in a heavy geometric sans ("We Capture What
  _Matters_").
- A row of three or four icon feature cards with generic copy
  ("Professional · Fast · Creative · Passionate").
- Stars/ratings, "3000+ customers" and avatar stacks as default hero filler
  (seen in R3, R4).
- Surreal clichés: monolith + moon + orbit ring + lone figure (seen in R5;
  acceptable only with a genuinely distinctive twist).
- Gibberish, lorem ipsum or placeholder text anywhere. It is the
  unmistakable sign of unreviewed AI output.
- Too many UI clusters competing in the hero (seen in R4).
- Borrowed brand trademarks or products as the hero.
- Purple-to-blue gradients, glowing blobs and centred hero + three cards.
- Every section fading up identically; uniform section rhythm.
- Generic stock imagery and AI imagery that hasn't been art-directed and
  graded into the site's world.

## 5. Principles for almost every premium website

These are the default standard. Deviate only with a stated reason.

1. **One concept per project.** Write it in one sentence before designing.
   Hero image, headline, motion and interaction must all argue it.
2. **One focal point per viewport** and a deliberate reading path.
3. **Scale contrast in typography**: display type far larger than body,
   with little in between. Body copy is quiet and precise.
4. **At most one voice shift per headline** (italic, script, weight or
   accent colour on a single word or phrase).
5. **A restrained palette**: a mostly neutral or tonal field and one accent
   (usually warm), used on under 5% of the surface and on what matters most.
6. **Light-led art direction**: choose the light (time, weather, source)
   and grade all imagery to it.
7. **Layered depth**: at least foreground, subject and background planes;
   integrate type into the layers where it serves the concept.
8. **Negative space as a material**: compact clusters with generous gaps
   between them.
9. **Specific, real content**: real names, numbers, places and imagery; no
   filler claims, placeholder or stock-feeling images.
10. **Custom-designed controls**: every button, link, nav, cursor state and
    scroll cue is designed for this brand.
11. **Minimal hero UI**: navigation, one primary action and optional
    edge metadata. Everything else moves below the fold.
12. **Motion reveals, never decorates**: slow, weighted, eased and
    choreographed by layer, with a meaningful reduced-motion equivalent
    (see CLAUDE.md).
13. **Legibility is sacred**: overlap and layering must never truncate or
    obscure the message (R4's error).
14. **Scroll is a narrative**: pace the page into chapters with a
    beginning, a build and a payoff.
15. **Continuity in transitions**: elements travel to where they're going.
16. **Mobile gets its own composition**: re-stage the layers, crop and
    type for a portrait frame; don't shrink the desktop layout.
17. **Run the swap test**: if the hero could be moved to another client by
    changing the logo, it isn't finished.
18. **Avoid every tell in §4**, whether or not it appears in a reference.

## 6. Techniques for specific clients only

Use a technique only when the concept, audience and budget call for it.

| --------------------------------------------------- | ---------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Monumental type behind the subject (masked/layered) | R2, R4     | Architecture, fashion, hospitality, strong single subjects with clean silhouettes             | The subject is busy or small; the text must be read in full              |
| Serif display + italic phrase                       | R3         | Home, lifestyle, wellness, hospitality, heritage, calm premium                                | Tech-forward, aggressive or youth brands                                 |
| Script + grotesk pairing                            | R2         | Craft, materials, artisanal, architecture, food                                               | Corporate, fintech, clinical                                             |
| Monospace-led typography                            | R1         | Deep tech, AI, developer tools, research, cultish launches                                    | Luxury retail, hospitality, anything warm                                |
| Dark cosmic/void canvas                             | R1, R5     | Launches, tech, entertainment, nightlife, high-drama brands                                   | Healthcare, family, food, anything that must feel open and friendly      |
| Tonal/monochrome world                              | R3         | Calm luxury, wellness, interiors, products with one hero object                               | Brands whose identity depends on several colours                         |
| Wireframe or stylised 3D                            | R1         | Tech concepts, "digital twin" or blueprint metaphors, where the concept is digital-meets-real | It is merely decorative                                                  |
| Interactive product/architectural 3D hero           | R3         | A tangible hero product or building the audience wants to explore                             | No real object to explore; low-end mobile audience; tight budget         |
| Surreal environment (arches, moons, water planes)   | R5         | Genuinely imaginative brands (creative studios, events, art), with a distinctive twist        | Almost always. It is the most clichéd AI aesthetic.                      |
| Real + rendered composite                           | R1         | Human ↔ technology stories                                                                    | The composite can't be executed convincingly                             |
| Hotspots on the hero image                          | R2         | Architecture, real estate, products with features, portfolios                                 | The image has nothing worth exploring                                    |
| Glass/frosted panel                                 | R2         | One small metadata card over rich imagery                                                     | As the whole aesthetic; stacked glass on glass                           |
| Ambient hero video (clouds, water, particles)       | R1, R2, R5 | The scene is naturally alive (weather, water, sky, crowds)                                    | It fights the LCP budget without a poster/fallback; reduced motion is on |

## 7. Using this benchmark

- **Before direction:** state which principles in §5 the concept expresses
  and which techniques in §6 it uses, with a reason for each.
- **During review:** check the output against §3 (expensive), §4 (human,
  plus the list of tells) and the swap test.
- **Never:** reproduce a reference's composition, subject, wordmark
  treatment or palette combination. Take the principle, then express it in
  the client's own world.
- **Keep it current:** when new references are added, add them to §1 and
  revise the principles only if the evidence genuinely changes.
